Measuring rain today

Measuring rain: where, and how often?

?
How is rain measured today? It depends on where, and how often. [Step 2] Rain gauges measure one point each: in Gothenburg, ten city gauges every minute, SMHI's gauge every 15 minutes, and nothing in between. [Step 3] Weather radar covers the whole area, in 2-kilometre boxes every 5 minutes, but from about 1.2 kilometres up, and with errors of its own. [Step 4] So this corner is empty: rain across the city, near the ground, every few seconds.

Why does that matter? This is Malmö in 2014, not this data set. Rain changes across a city: in Gothenburg, in this data set, over two days, some gauges caught a few millimetres and others up to 50. [Step 2] And it changes fast: small urban basins respond in under an hour. Flood forecasts and sewer control need rain seen densely and quickly.

Phone networks

Microwave links: a third view.

> 20 000 link hops in Sweden · over 400 000 in Europe

Ericsson (2018), cited in §5

≥ 10per km² sub-links in the heart of Gothenburg

§2.1, Fig. 5b

This is where phone networks come in. Operators connect their masts with microwave links that cross the whole city, about 30 metres up. In the pilot behind this paper, every node was polled every 10 seconds. The paper calls them a powerful complement to gauges and radar. [Step 2] And they already exist: more than 20 000 link hops in Sweden, over 400 000 in Europe, and downtown at least ten sub-links per square kilometre.

The phenomenon

Why does rain dim a radio link?

Above about 10 GHz, rain attenuates a radio signal, more at higher frequency

§1 · these links: 7–38 GHz, mostly above 25 GHz (§2.1)

You may have seen this at home: in a downpour, satellite TV loses its picture. Here, in heavy rain, one dish receives two channels: at 4 gigahertz the picture still decodes, at 12 gigahertz its quality drops to zero. [Step 2] The same physics acts on these links, at 7 to 38 gigahertz. Above about 10 gigahertz, water drops attenuate a radio signal, and the higher the frequency, the more. The links are planned to keep working through rain, but their received signal drops.

The idea

Rain dims the link.

Link 10227 at Torslanda, 1.8 km: one end sends, the other receives.

≈ 1/500 of the power, still received

Here is one of those links: link 10227 at Torslanda, 1.8 kilometres long. One end sends, the other receives. This is an illustration: when rain falls on the path, the received power drops. (Drag the rain across the link; scroll to make it rain harder.) [Step 2] And this is the real thing, measured on this link on 28 July 2015. [Step 3] As the storm passed, the received level fell about 27 dB, to minus 73.6 dBm, at 15:06, one minute after the gauge beside it recorded 1.1 millimetres in a single minute. [Step 4] That is about a five-hundredth of the power, and the signal was still received.

How is the signal turned into rain? The paper describes the general approach most methods follow. Filter out erroneous values; the attenuation is what was sent minus what was received. [Step 2] Classify each step as wet or dry; the dry level comes from the dry steps around it, and only the fade above it, in wet steps, can be rain. [Step 3] That fade per kilometre grows with the rain rate by a power law that depends on frequency and polarization; inverted, with corrections for non-rain effects such as wet antennas, it gives the rain rate. (Scroll to change the frequency.) [Step 4] Finally, many crossing links are interpolated into a rain map. On the right, each link's measured fade at 15:06, against its own median: our simple baseline, not the paper's method. The paper describes this approach; it does not apply it.

So why isn't everyone doing this? The data. Link data usually stay with the operators and the groups they work with. [Step 2] The few open sets have either a handful of links, or thousands of links but only 15-minute statistics. [Step 3] OpenMRG opens 364 links, every 10 seconds, with true coordinates, plus the gauges and the radar, for the same three months.

Here is that network: 364 links, 728 sub-links, 418 nodes, 1041 kilometres of paths. [Step 2] Short links run at high frequency, mostly downtown; long ones at low frequency, outside. Higher frequencies are kept short so that links stay robust in rain. [Step 3] Four terms. A node is a mast. A sub-link is one direction, sender to receiver; a link is both directions; a hop is all links between two nodes.

How were the data collected? A package written for this pilot polled every node with standard SNMP: 150 at a time, all likely within a second, every 10 seconds, no firmware changes. (On the map, the polling order is our guess.) [Step 2] Each reading carries the start of its 10-second window. Every minute the data went to Ericsson's server; Ericsson forwarded them to SMHI. Just over a minute from rain to data. [Step 3] The load was tiny: about 10 bytes per second per node, 4180 for the whole network. [Step 4] One text message every 14 seconds; the network needed less than a 56k modem.

One storm, seen by all three sensors: 28 July 2015 at Torslanda. On top the gauge, in the middle link 10227, below the radar cell over the gauge. At 14:57:20 the link records its first decrease; the gauge's first record comes a minute or two later, a small difference, likely from where each sensor sits and how the storm moved. [Step 2] The peak: 1.1 millimetres in one minute at the gauge, and a minute later the deepest fade of sub-link 115, about 27 dB. [Step 3] And the radar? At the peak, the cell over the gauge showed no rain, but heavier rain a few kilometres north. The reason is unknown; misalignment is the most likely. [Step 4] Afterwards the gauge reads zero at 15:22 while the link is still recovering: likely wet antennas, its position, or because a link sees its whole path. At 16:22 the same 0.7 millimetres a minute as in the first peak fades it less; that could be alignment, or antennas wetter earlier. (Scroll to move through the storm.)

The collection worked. 99.99 percent of the 10-second steps had data from at least one sub-link; the SMHI gauge 100 percent, the radar 99.6; and 93 percent of the sub-links delivered data at least 99 percent of the time. [Step 2] Only 51 steps had no link data at all: about eight and a half minutes in three months.

The summer in numbers. Received levels concentrate around the mean, likely dry, stable conditions; the rain information lies in the left tail: each sub-link's lowest reading is on average about 30 dB below its median, and rain is a main cause. [Step 2] The radar shows mostly stratiform rain, a few values above the convective threshold of 40 dBZ, and a small peak that might be clutter. [Step 3] The gauges recorded about 260 millimetres over the summer; the city weighing gauges saw rain in about 6 percent of 15-minute intervals. The tipping buckets' mean is distinctly lower: 219 against 263 millimetres.
