Hi everyone. This is MoiréDeform: towards fine-grained deformation sensing through everyday moiré, from the weave of an ordinary chair.

Everyday deformation

We deform what holds us.

Sitting, leaning, breathing: each one bends the mesh.

The small ones, like a breath, are hard to observe without instrumenting the surface or the user.

Dent drawn exaggerated

Every time we sit, lean or breathe, we bend the surfaces that hold us. Those deformations carry our posture, and even our breathing. [Step 2] But the small ones, like a breath, are hard to observe, unless you instrument the surface or the user: the way we strap a chest belt on, just to get our reference.

Moiré can help. Take two fine patterns with slightly different periods: slide one by a hair, and the bands sweep many times as far. (Move across the gratings.) [Step 2] Our version uses one pattern at two moments: before and after a press. Subtract them, and fringes appear where the lines moved. (Move over the middle panel.)

Our insight

Let the weave be the grating.

Camera view · live − reference · simulated

Fringes · shift further

So we let the weave itself be the grating. A camera behind the chair keeps one frame of the empty backrest, and compares every live frame with it. The weave shifts only a little; the fringes shift further. [Step 2] This is the real difference view from our camera, with a person seated and breathing.

The hardware is one low-cost monochrome camera on a clip, behind an unmodified mesh chair: 1280 by 800 pixels, at 30 frames a second, with a laptop processing the video. No markers, and no added gratings. [Step 2] And it only sees the chair back: the person sitting in the chair is never in frame.

Larger deformations become input. We take the pattern's envelope in the mesh's frequency band; its weighted centroid marks where you lean. (Move over the backrest to lean.) [Step 2] Normalize it to the sensing region, subtract your calibrated neutral pose, and you have a continuous 2D cursor. [Step 3] In the demo, leaning alone drives the on-screen joystick; visitors will steer it along guided paths.

Breathing is smaller: it may barely move the whole-field centroid. So the fine path tracks the moiré pattern's subpixel shift in a central band: contrast equalization, temporal band-pass amplification, phase correlation, then a causal band-pass filter. [Step 2] A lightweight 1D CNN turns 15-second windows of that signal into a breathing waveform: here in green, live, against a chest belt worn only for comparison. [Step 3] When the signal's amplitude before the CNN falls well below the user's calibrated breathing level, the system suppresses the CNN's output, so a held breath stays flat.

Leave-one-session-out

Does the moiré itself help? With the same camera, on the same recordings, the raw moiré signal's breathing-rate error, averaged over three sessions, was 0.71 breaths per minute, against 2.07 for tracking a physical marker. [Step 2] Across ten sessions from five users, the session-level breathing rate was within 0.72, plus or minus 0.45, breaths per minute of the chest belt, and the waveform correlated with the belt at 0.69, leave-one-session-out.
