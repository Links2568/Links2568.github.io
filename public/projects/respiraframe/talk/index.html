Breathing says a lot about us. Coughs, sniffs, blowing the nose, sneezes and clearing the throat are respiratory events worth tracking in daily life. [Step 2] So are the patterns of ordinary breathing: through the nose or the mouth, deep or shallow, and when we hold our breath.

Our idea

Room noise · the frame's sensor picks up less of it; impedance is not acoustic

Electrodesnose pads, temples

Drawn, slowed down

Head: Lee Perry-Smith scan · CC BY 3.0

The idea: eyeglasses sit right next to the nose and mouth, and touch the face at the nose pads and the temples. So one frame can carry two channels. First, bone conduction: a vibration sensor, the VPU, embedded beside a nose pad, picks up breathing and coughing as vibrations carried through the face and the rigid frame, rather than relying on the air. [Step 2] Second, impedance: eight dry electrodes where the frame touches the face send tiny currents, and the face's impedance changes as we breathe. [Step 3] Room noise travels through the air: the frame's sensor picks up less of it, and impedance is not acoustic at all. Two channels, with different weaknesses.

Here are the sensors. Listen: the VPU, embedded in a notch near the nose pad, with a bandwidth up to 8 kilohertz. [Step 2] Feel: eight dry electrodes, two on each nose pad and two at each temple tip, driven with a 50-kilohertz, 400-microamp current. [Step 3] In the prototype, a separate sensing board acquires both channels and streams them wirelessly.

Does bone conduction really hear less of the room? These are the paper's own recordings from a bone-conduction microphone and a conventional one; compare each with itself. Under 80 decibels the microphone fills with noise around the cough; the bone-conduction picture barely changes. (Scroll to step through.) With noise alone, the microphone fills up and the bone-conduction picture stays nearly featureless. In the paper's words, bone conduction is less sensitive to ambient noise, while keeping the respiratory cues. [Step 2] How the sensor is mounted matters too: touching the skin, inside a hollow frame, or embedded in it. Embedded, the frame works as a passive acoustic barrier: the best balance of the three, though some leakage remains.

The second channel uses tiny currents. A neighbouring pair of electrodes injects a 50-kilohertz current, the other neighbouring pairs measure, and the injecting pair rotates around the frame. (Point at an electrode to inject there.) [Step 2] That gives 64 measurement channels; the 40 that carry data are drawn here, and breathing changes them. These are 4-second windows from the dataset, on one scale: deep or shallow breathing, through the nose or the mouth. (Click a class.)

Fusion

Each task's model listens to both.

Four tasks, four such models

Fused vs the weaker channel: p < 0.001 · the paper's bars carry ±7–10-point error bars, not drawn here

For each task, a model listens to both channels. The VPU's spectrogram, and the EIT's channels, smoothed, differenced and smoothed again, each go through a small CNN. [Step 2] Attention fuses the two, and a classifier decides. There are four such models, one per task. [Step 3] Neither channel wins everywhere. On each user's own data, impedance does better on nasal versus oral and deep versus shallow breathing; the VPU on abnormal events and breath holding. Fused, it is significantly better than the weaker channel on every task.

Leave-one-participant-out

In the lab, with 19 participants and models trained on each person's own sessions, RespiraFrame recognizes abnormal respiratory events at 92.5 percent, nasal versus oral breathing at 84.9, deep versus shallow at 88.0, and, for the 15 who did that task, breath holding at 87.8 percent. The clips are the live demos of each task. [Step 2] For people the model has never seen, the accuracies are 80.6, 75.4, 81.6 and 87.8 percent.

Then the hard conditions. Noise: recorded noise up to 80 decibels was added to the VPU's test data; from 40 to 80 decibels, accuracy drops by less than two to three points. [Step 2] Wind: from a fan to a hair dryer on full, abnormal events fall from 85 to 80 to 69 percent, while the other tasks show no significant change. The paper calls the strongest level a stress test, uncommon day to day. [Step 3] A bystander: while the person beside the wearer has one of the five events, and the wearer only talks, drinks, tidies up, uses a phone or breathes, 2.7 percent of those recordings are taken for the wearer's own events. [Step 4] Motion: breathing route stays at 93 to 94 percent standing, walking or running in place; abnormal events fall from 87 to 70 percent when running.

Outside the lab, ten participants wore it in an office, a cafeteria and outdoors, on different days from the training data. Averaged over the settings, abnormal events reached 87.6 percent, nasal versus oral 81.5, and deep versus shallow 84.6.
